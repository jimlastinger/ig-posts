## @applied_stoic slot 1: Marcus 6.30, "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar" (the promotion)
Source: https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.6.six.html (tr. George Long). Cite "Marcus Aurelius · Meditations 6.30 · tr. George Long".
The page labels this passage as section 30 of Book Six (29 begins "It is a shame for the soul to be first to give way in this life."; 31 begins "Return to thy sober senses and call thyself back.").
Verbatim, opening of 6.30 (consecutive; checked word by word against the page Oct 12 — the page prints "notdyed", a run-together typo; write "not dyed"):
"Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen. Keep thyself then simple, good, pure, serious, free from affectation, a friend of justice, a worshipper of the gods, kind, affectionate, strenuous in all proper acts. Strive to continue to be such as philosophy wished to make thee. Reverence the gods, and help men. Short is life. There is only one fruit of this terrene life, a pious disposition and social acts."
Also verbatim, later in 6.30: "Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus."
- Context you may state: the rest of 6.30 holds up Antoninus (Antoninus Pius, Marcus's adoptive father and the emperor before him) as a model of steadiness and modesty. Don't quote the rest of 6.30; it wasn't verified word for word.
- "Caesar" here = being changed by the imperial title/power. Modern angle: the promotion, the new title, the first time you manage people. Exercise: write down who you were before the title; ask a peer if you've changed.

## @applied_stoic slot 2: Epictetus on comparing yourself to others
Source: https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html (tr. Elizabeth Carter). Cite "Epictetus · Enchiridion 44 · tr. Elizabeth Carter" (and "Enchiridion 25").
Enchiridion 44, complete (verbatim; confirmed IDENTICAL against the page Oct 12):
"These reasonings are unconnected: "I am richer than you, therefore I am better"; "I am more eloquent than you, therefore I am better." The connection is rather this: "I am richer than you, therefore my property is greater than yours;" "I am more eloquent than you, therefore my style is better than yours." But you, after all, are neither property nor style."
Enchiridion 25, opening (verbatim; confirmed IDENTICAL Oct 12):
"Is anyone preferred before you at an entertainment, or in a compliment, or in being admitted to a consultation? If these things are good, you ought to be glad that he has gotten them; and if they are evil, don't be grieved that you have not gotten them."
- Don't use 013-insulted's line (Ench. 20) or Ench. 33 ("make comparisons", used Oct 13).
- Modern angle: scrolling a former classmate's promotion post, comparing salaries, cars, houses. Exercise: when you catch a comparison, rewrite it Epictetus's way ("their car is nicer than mine" — not "they are better than me").

  (Also from the same table: 2015 0.0, 2016 0.3, 2017 2.0, 2018 2.8, 2019 1.6. A 0.0 year means no raise.)
  Timing (latestCOLA): the 2.8% applies "beginning with the December 2025 benefits", which are paid in January 2026. SSI also rose 2.8% for January 2026. Press release: Social Security increases begin in January 2026, and SSI increases begin "on December 31, 2025."
How COLA is calculated (verbatim):
- latestCOLA: "COLAs are based on increases in the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W)." The third-quarter (Jul-Sep) CPI-W average for the current year is compared with the Q3 average from the last year a COLA took effect. For 2025 that was 308.729 (2024 Q3 base) against 317.265 (2025 Q3 average), which gives 2.8 percent.
- colaseries: "After 1983, COLAs have been based on increases in the CPI-W from the third quarter of the prior year to the corresponding quarter of the current year in which the COLA became effective."
- Press release: "The Social Security Act ties the annual COLA to the increase in the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W) as determined by the Department of Labor's Bureau of Labor Statistics."
- colaseries: since 1975, general benefit increases have been COLAs. "Prior to 1975, Social Security benefit increases were set by legislation."
Context only, not a forecast: BLS August 2026 CPI says CPI-W "increased 3.5 percent over the last 12 months", and CPI-U all items rose 3.4% before seasonal adjustment (released Sept 11, 2026). This is NOT the COLA and must not be presented as one.
2026 numbers (SSA 2026 COLA Fact Sheet):
- Average monthly benefit, all retired workers: $2,015 before the COLA, $2,071 after (estimated, January 2026)
- Aged couple, both receiving benefits: $3,120 -> $3,208. All disabled workers: $1,586 -> $1,630. Aged widow(er) alone: $1,867 -> $1,919
- Max taxable earnings (wage base): $176,100 (2025) -> $184,500 (2026). Employee tax rate 7.65%, self-employed 15.30%, both unchanged
- Quarter of coverage: $1,810 -> $1,890
- Earnings test, under full retirement age: $23,400/yr ($1,950/mo) -> $24,480/yr ($2,040/mo). In the year you reach FRA: $62,160/yr -> $65,160/yr
- Max benefit, worker retiring at full retirement age: $4,018/mo -> $4,152/mo
- SSI federal payment standard: individual $967 -> $994/mo; couple $1,450 -> $1,491/mo
- Press release: the 2.8% raise reaches "75 million Americans" (nearly 71 million Social Security beneficiaries and nearly 7.5 million SSI recipients)
Our math:
- The 2.8% raise works out to +$56/month for the average retired worker ($2,071 - $2,015), about $672/yr. SSA's after-COLA figures are estimates.
- 317.265 / 308.729 = 1.0276, i.e. 2.76%, which SSA rounds to 2.8% (rounding to the nearest tenth is our inference from the numbers; SSA does not state it on that page).
- Compounded, the 2022-2026 raises (5.9, 8.7, 3.2, 2.5, 2.8) give about +25.2% (1.059 x 1.087 x 1.032 x 1.025 x 1.028 = 1.2518). Including the 2021 raise (1.3%), 2021-2026 compounds to about +26.8%. Say "about".
- Wage base rose $8,400 ($184,500 - $176,100). Max FRA benefit rose $134/mo.
Hook idea: "Your Social Security raise is set by 3 months of prices." Then a slide of 2021-2026 raises (1.3 / 5.9 / 8.7 / 3.2 / 2.5 / 2.8). Then "where the number comes from" (CPI-W, Jul-Sep average vs last year). Close with "the 2027 number depends on September prices; we'll post it when SSA does."
Don'ts:

## @dollartrail slot 2: A $300 NYC hotel night: the taxes you pay that locals don't
Sources:
- https://www.nyc.gov/site/finance/business/business-hotel-room-occupancy-tax.page (NYC Dept. of Finance, "Business Hotel Room Occupancy Tax"; no page date, footer (c) 2025; fetched Oct 12, 2026)
- https://www.nyc.gov/assets/finance/downloads/pdf/08pdf/fm3608htx.pdf (NYC DOF Finance Memorandum 08-1REV, Nov 12, 2021, "Guidance for Hotel Operators Subject to the New York City Tax on Hotel Occupancy")
- https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-02794 (NYC311 "Hotel Room Occupancy Tax"; footer (c) 2026)
- https://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/hotel_and_motel_occupancy.htm (NYS TB-ST-331, issued July 30, 2025, updated July 9, 2026)
- https://www.tax.ny.gov/e-services/stmi/sch_n.htm (NYS Schedule N, Selected Sales and Services in New York City; no page date)
- https://www.tax.ny.gov/pdf/publications/sales/pub718.pdf (Publication 718 (2/25), "Effective March 1, 2025")
- https://www.tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/tg_bulletins/st/sales_tax_rates_additional_sales_taxes_and_fees.htm (NYS TB-ST-825, page footer "Updated: April 6, 2026")
Every tax and fee on a NYC hotel room night (stays under 90 days):
1. NYS + NYC sales tax, 8 7/8% (8.875%) of the room rent
   - Schedule N: "For the first 90 days of room occupancy: at the state and local rate of 8⅞%." and "For the 91st through 180th days of room occupancy: at the 4½% local tax, only."
   - Pub 718: "New York City 8⅞" (reporting code 8081). Footnote: "Rates in these jurisdictions include 3/8% imposed for the benefit of the Metropolitan Commuter Transportation District." TB-ST-825: state rate "currently 4%", MCTD "⅜% (0.375%)".
   - (Our math: 8.875% - 4% state - 0.375% MCTD = 4.5% NYC local, which matches Schedule N's "4½% local tax".)
2. NYC Hotel Room Occupancy Tax (city, NYC Dept. of Finance), 5.875% of rent
   - NYC311: "The Hotel Room Occupancy Tax rate of 5.875% went into effect on December 20, 2013."
   - DOF memo 08-1REV: "Rent is taxed at a rate of 5.875 % of the amount of rent charged for the use or occupancy of a room."
3. NYC hotel tax flat per-room charge, on top of the 5.875% (DOF memo / DOF page; rent per day):
   - Less than $10: $0.00 | $10 to under $20: $0.50 per day | $20 to under $30: $1.00 per day | $30 to under $40: $1.50 per day | "$40 or more $2.00 per day"
   - DOF page: "$2.00 per day per room + the hotel room occupancy tax rate" for rent of "$40 or more".
4. NYC hotel unit fee, $1.50 per unit per day (collected by NYS)
   - TB-ST-331: operators and booking services must "charge a unit fee of $1.50 per unit per day of occupancy in New York City." "The New York City unit fee is not subject to state and local sales taxes."
   - TB-ST-825: "a hotel unit fee in the amount of $1.50 per unit per day applies to hotels located in New York City."
Javits/convention center fee: no official page we checked mentions one, and none says what the $1.50 unit fee funds. Don't call it a "Javits fee".
Who's exempt ("locals"):
- NYS sales tax: a permanent resident must stay "at least 90 consecutive days" (TB-ST-331). In NYC, "the local sales tax applies until a guest has stayed for at least 180 consecutive days."
- NYC hotel tax: "A permanent resident (one who occupies a room for at least 180 consecutive days)" (DOF page). NYC311: "A permanent resident who lives in the building more than 180 days."
- Accurate framing: these taxes fall on hotel guests. NYC residents don't pay them in daily life (rent on a home isn't taxed this way). A New Yorker who books a hotel night pays them too.
Worked example, one night at a $300 room rate (our math; ILLUSTRATIVE, label it). Assumes each percentage tax applies to the $300 room rent only. The official pages we read don't spell out how the two taxes stack, so say "about" and "before any hotel/resort fees":
- Sales tax 8.875% x $300 = $26.625 -> $26.63
- NYC hotel room occupancy tax 5.875% x $300 = $17.625 -> $17.63
- NYC hotel tax per-room charge (rent $40+): $2.00
- NYC hotel unit fee: $1.50
- Total taxes and fees: $47.75 exact (about $47.76 if each line is rounded to cents) = about 15.9% of $300. Night total about $347.75.
- Percent taxes combine to 14.75% of rent, plus $3.50 flat per night.
- Flat fees are a bigger share on cheap rooms: on a $100 room they'd be $14.75 + $3.50 = $18.25 = 18.25% (our math, same assumptions).
Hook idea: "Book a $300 hotel room in NYC. You'll pay about $348." Then one slide per line (state + city sales tax / city hotel tax / $2 room charge / $1.50 unit fee), ending with "stay 180 days and most of it goes away."
Don'ts:
- Don't say "Javits fee" or say what the $1.50 fee funds (unverified).
- Don't include resort/destination/booking fees. Those aren't taxes.
- Don't call the total exact for every booking; label it illustrative.
- Don't say "locals are exempt" without the context above: residency exemptions run on 90/180 consecutive days, not on where you live.
- The 5.875% rate started Dec 20, 2013. No official effective date is shown for the $2.00 charge or the $1.50 unit fee, so don't give dates for them.
